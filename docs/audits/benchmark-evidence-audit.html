Which objections hold up, and what a better report looks like

The written audit lists 29 findings and rates most of them high. Read soberly, five are errors anyone would recognize from the papers' own numbers. The rest are limits on what the results can claim, questions the authors could answer, or points that are easy to overstate. This page separates those, shows each headline problem visually, and closes with how our own testing would avoid them.

No new benchmark runs were performed for the audit. Ratings on this page are our re-assessment of the audit's findings and each carries a one-line verification step. Sponsorship is disclosed in every publication and is not treated as evidence of wrong results.

How to read the ratings

Four buckets, with the audit's own rating shown beside ours

Confirmed error

A number, unit, command, or label is inconsistent with the paper's own evidence. Verifiable without new runs.

Real limitation

The design cannot support a claim the paper makes or invites. The measurements may still be correct.

Open question

Two disclosed items do not reconcile, or a detail is missing. Needs an answer from the authors, not an accusation.

Minor or overstated

A naming issue, a disclosed choice, or a useful extra experiment the authors never promised.

The bar we hold ourselves to. A critical finding can block one comparative claim without invalidating a paper. Missing tuning detail is a reproducibility problem, not proof of bad tuning. A measured gain needs a baseline and an improved configuration on the same workload, with quality and latency held to declared limits. Every objection below is stated so that it could be aimed at our own report.

Paper D · Dell PowerEdge R770 with RTX PRO 4500 · Qwen models

Two errors visible from the paper's own numbers

Useful load curves and some real placement detail. But the efficiency arithmetic mixes scopes, and the stated BF16 configuration cannot physically fit on the stated GPUs. Both must be fixed before any figure from this paper is reused.

Sober verdict
Fix before reuse

Throughput curves may be fine. The efficiency headline is wrong by construction, and the configuration text is wrong or incomplete.

2 confirmed errors
D2

Aggregate throughput divided by one GPU's power

Confirmed error

What the paper doesThe 8B discussion divides 5,219 tokens/s by 151 W and reports 34.5 tokens/s/W, then talks about multiplying efficiency across four replicas.

Why anyone would objectIf the throughput is the total of four GPUs, the power must be the total of four GPUs. If both are per GPU, adding replicas cannot change the ratio at all. Either way the published figure does not follow.

Audit ratedCriticalWe rateConfirmed error

We agree. It is pure arithmetic on the paper's own numbers. Scope is narrow: it breaks the efficiency claims, not the throughput curves.

Verify in ten minutes. Re-divide the published token rate by the published per-GPU power times replica count. Check whether any summary table uses a different denominator than the prose.

What we do insteadIntegrate wall power over exactly the window in which tokens are counted. Keep GPU-only energy as a separate metric. Report tokens per joule, and have one script recompute every table from the raw counters.

D1

BF16 weights that cannot fit the stated 32 GB GPU

Confirmed inconsistency

What the paper doesThe methodology describes BF16 and four TP1 replicas on 32 GB GPUs, including 27B and 35B models.

Why anyone would objectTwo bytes per parameter puts the 27B weights at roughly 54 GB and the 35B weights at 70 GB. Neither fits one 32 GB card, and a TP1 replica cannot borrow memory from its neighbours.

Audit ratedCriticalWe rateConfirmed inconsistency

The write-up is wrong as written. The most likely explanation is a documentation error and a quantized or offloaded deployment. That does not make the measured outputs invented, but it does mean nobody can say what configuration produced them.

Verify in an hour. Confirm the exact wording on the methodology page, check the model cards for parameter counts, and look for any mention of offload, FP8, or NVFP4 elsewhere in the chapters.

What we do insteadBefore launch, estimate weights, KV cache, activations, and headroom, then verify against the observed allocation. Record the actual dtype and kernels from the running engine, not the model name. A mismatch blocks publication.

Paper C · Prowess Consulting · Xeon 6 workload comparison

A wrong command, a wrong table, and an honest confounder

Prowess published concrete commands, which is why its problems are findable. The NAMD launcher does not do what the text says, the web table disagrees with the PDF, and the platforms differ in core count and memory. None of this shows the measurements are false.

Sober verdict
Numbers probably survive; reproduction does not

The aggregate results are likely real. The per-core story, the exact NAMD placement, and one headline percentage need repair.

2 confirmed errors
C4

numactl -C takes CPU IDs, not a core count

Confirmed command error

What the paper doesThe published NAMD launcher binds with numactl -C $NUM_CPU_CORES, looping over thread counts.

Why anyone would objectAnyone who has used numactl knows -C is a CPU list. The command as written does not allocate the intended disjoint cores, so the description of the experiment and the experiment itself disagree.

Audit ratedHighWe rateConfirmed, impact unknown

Confirmed as a command error. Not confirmed as a result error: NAMD can set its own affinity, and a run genuinely squeezed onto one CPU would have been visibly terrible. Most likely the real script differed from the published one. That is a reproducibility failure, not evidence of a wrong number.

Verify in an hour. Run the published loop on any Linux host and record each thread's affinity. Ask Prowess for the concurrent ensemble launcher they actually used.

What we do insteadGenerate explicit CPU lists per job, bind memory to the intended NUMA node, and capture every worker thread's effective affinity during the run. Publish the concurrent launcher, not a sequential loop.

C2

More job slots, not faster cores

Real limitation

What the paper doesCompares systems with different total cores and memory configuration, then reports an aggregate gain of roughly 84 to 87 percent.

Why anyone would objectThirty jobs against sixteen is 1.875 times the slots. An 85 percent aggregate gain is what you get from more slots at equal per-job speed. It says nothing about core architecture.

Audit ratedHighWe rateReal limitation

Slightly overstated by the audit. The paper acknowledges the per-job distinction and the aggregate result is a valid full-system capacity comparison. It becomes a problem only when sales material calls it a per-core or architecture gain.

Verify in ten minutes. Divide the reported aggregate gain by the ratio of job slots. Check whether any derived material drops the word "aggregate".

What we do insteadReport full-system capacity and a matched-core, matched-job comparison side by side, with memory population stated. Throughput per job, per core, per system, and energy per unit of work must reconcile.

C6

152 percent in the PDF became 52 percent on the web

Confirmed discrepancy

What the paper doesThe original PDF's Table 2 shows 152 percent for the INT8 batch-one maximum. The web version shows 52 percent, with inconsistent direction labels.

Why anyone would objectA reader who sees both versions cannot know which number the vendor stands behind. If either is copied into a sales deck, the deck is wrong half the time.

Audit ratedMediumWe rateConfirmed error, medium impact

Agree with the audit. Clearly an error, clearly a publishing one. It matters because the web page is what customers find first.

Verify in five minutes. Open the PDF table as an image and the web page side by side. Note which direction each label claims.

What we do insteadGenerate web, Word, and PDF tables from one calculation source with automatic direction and min/max checks. A release passes only when every format agrees with the source data.

Paper E · Principled Technologies · agentic AI on Dell hardware

A reversed chart label and cost arithmetic that needs a bridge

PT discloses workflow structure, software versions, topology, and cost assumptions, and some workflows are genuinely agentic. The headline economics chart contradicts its own appendix, and the token counts behind the cost and energy figures do not reconcile without information the report does not give.

Sober verdict
Ask for the workbook

One confirmed labelling error. The larger accounting questions are exactly that: questions. The audit is right to say they are not evidence of fraud.

1 confirmed error 2 open questions
E1

The cost chart and the appendix table point in opposite directions

Confirmed contradiction

What the paper doesFigure 5 labels the lower per-token cost with lower utilization. Appendix Table 3 assigns the values the other way round.

Why anyone would objectSpreading a fixed acquisition cost over more output lowers cost per token. A chart that says the opposite of its own appendix undermines the headline economic claim regardless of which is right.

Audit ratedHighWe rateConfirmed error, likely cosmetic

Confirmed, and the appendix is almost certainly the correct version. The underlying numbers probably stand. The damage is to credibility, which for a headline economics figure is the point.

Verify in five minutes. Report page 10 against appendix page 5. Confirm the direction that matches the cost formula.

What we do insteadEvery chart is generated from the same calculation table as the appendix. A stale caption or reversed label fails an automated check before release.

E2

The cost total implies 31 times the headline token rate

Open question

What the paper doesPublishes a five-year cloud cost of $5,180,423 at $0.90 per million tokens, and a headline rate of 1,179 tokens/s.

Why anyone would objectThe two numbers describe very different token volumes. A reader trying to reproduce the savings claim cannot get from one to the other.

Audit ratedHighWe rateOpen question

The audit says itself this is "not necessarily an error", and we agree. Input plus output tokens across a mixed workload can easily be an order of magnitude above an output-only headline. It becomes a finding only if PT cannot produce the bridge. The same applies to E3, where 0.1816 kWh beside 144 J per million tokens implies about 4.5 billion tokens with no stated window.

Verify by asking. Request input, cached input, reasoning, and output token counts by workflow, plus the utilization and availability assumptions in the cost model.

What we do insteadPublish token counts by category and workload, show capacity, utilization, and availability assumptions, and label a calculated cloud bill as a calculation. Every total reconciles to the workload within rounding.

Paper A · Dell · Llama inference comparison across GPU platforms

No errors found. Several variables move at once.

A useful configuration comparison across request lengths and load levels, and unusually candid about which repeats were selected. The problem is causal: precision, serving profile, placement, and hardware all change together, so the results compare configurations rather than isolating a GPU.

Sober verdict
Read as a configuration comparison

Sound measurements with a narrower meaning than a reader might take from them. Whether that is a problem depends on the exact claim Dell makes.

A1

Precision, platform, and parallelism change together

Real limitation

What the paper doesCompares NVFP4 and FP8 configurations across different GPU platforms, with tensor parallelism left uncontrolled.

Why anyone would objectYou changed the precision and the hardware and the parallelism, and you are crediting one of them. Any engineer will ask which one.

Audit ratedHighWe rateReal limitation

High only if Dell claims a hardware-architecture gain. As a "best qualified configuration per platform" comparison it is legitimate and useful. The fix is a sentence naming which kind of claim it is, plus the unavoidable differences listed beside the result.

Verify in twenty minutes. Read the exact claim wording on the software-stack and cross-metric pages. Note also that the NVFP4 label does not prove native FP4 execution: NVIDIA documents an emulation path on earlier GPUs.

What we do insteadTwo tracks. A controlled track fixes model revision, precision, workload, serving stack, and quality gates. A deployment track gives each platform the same tuning budget and compares its best qualified configuration. Every claim names its track.

A4

The curve is still rising at the last tested point

Real limitation

What the paper doesShort-context throughput curves stop at the top tested concurrency while still climbing. A decline on another profile is attributed to hardware.

Why anyone would objectIf the line is still going up when you stop, you have not found the top. And a decline needs queue, memory, and client telemetry before it is blamed on the GPU.

Audit ratedHighWe rateReal limitation

A boundary problem, not an error. Only critical if the figure is repeated as "maximum" in sizing guidance. The remaining Llama findings (selection of representative repeats, missing NIM profile, average latency) are disclosure matters; the disclosed run selection in particular is honesty, not cherry-picking.

Verify in ten minutes. Check the slope between the last two concurrency points on each chart and whether any text uses "maximum" or "peak".

What we do insteadExtend the load sweep adaptively until two successive steps gain under 3 percent, or state that the search boundary was reached and report a lower bound. Mark the highest load that still meets the latency target as a separate point.

B2

The cohort shrinks as context grows, so the average changes meaning

Real limitation

What the paper doesReports accuracy trends across context lengths while the number of evaluated configurations decreases as context grows. Variants of one family are counted as separate samples.

Why anyone would objectA trend line through a shrinking group can rise or fall purely because weaker or stronger models dropped out. That is a familiar survivorship trap.

Audit ratedHighWe rateReal limitation

Genuine, and cheap to fix with a paired common-cohort analysis. It limits the degradation-with-length conclusion, not the per-model scores. Closely related is B3: a leaderboard without intervals cannot show whether close rankings mean anything.

Verify in twenty minutes. Count configurations per context length on physical pages 7 and 16. Recompute the trend on the subset present at every length.

What we do insteadPublish a paired common-cohort analysis beside the all-eligible one. Bootstrap by corpus or seed, report intervals and ties, and use the same document bytes for paired comparisons. Unsupported context lengths are marked unsupported, not scored.

How we do better

Every problem above falls into one of three failures: not testing enough of the space, not understanding what was actually running, or not leaving a trail others can follow. Our report is built to pass each test.

Thoroughness

Test enough of the space that the claim is bracketed, and keep every cell, including the ones that failed.

  • Sweep to saturation or say so. Load rises until two steps gain under 3 percent; otherwise report a lower bound, never a maximum. A4
  • Both loop types. Closed-loop concurrency and open-loop arrival rates at 25 to 110 percent of pilot capacity, plus bursts. A5 · E5
  • Adequate samples for tails. At least 10,000 requests behind any headline p99, with its uncertainty. Percentiles are never averaged across runs.
  • Confirmation, not best-of. Screen at most 24 candidates, confirm the best two on fresh seeds in three randomized paired blocks, five if variance exceeds 5 percent. A3
  • Full matrix published. Failed cells, below-sample cells, and infeasible configurations stay in the evidence index. D5
  • Sustained runs. Two hours for a finalist, 24 hours when reliability is part of the claim, with controlled replica loss and recovery timing.

Understanding

Expert knowledge of what is actually running, what limits it, and what a change would do, before any number is attributed to anything.

  • Know it fits before it runs. Estimate weights, KV cache, activations, and headroom; verify against observed allocation; record the real dtype and kernels, not the model name. D1 · A2
  • Map the machine. Logical CPU to core to NUMA node, PCIe width and generation, GPU peer access, NIC placement, effective affinity of every worker. C4 · E4
  • One knob at a time. Change one factor, then combine winners. Equal tuning budgets per platform. A changed precision belongs to the deployment track, not a hardware gain. A1
  • Diagnose before blaming. Split cold load, compilation, warm prefill, queue delay, first token, and reasoning tokens before calling a delay "batching". Trace stages before calling CPU utilization "the bottleneck". D3 · E6
  • Name the operating point. Peak throughput and interactive service are different points on the same curve. Every recommendation says which one it is. D4
  • Respect the model's limits. TP divisibility, supported context, tokenizer differences, and what active parameters do and do not mean in a MoE model.

Reproducibility

Another operator can launch the same configuration, regenerate every chart, and get the same conclusions, including the unflattering ones.

  • Frozen manifest per run. Server, BIOS, firmware, GPU power limits, driver, container digest, engine revision, model and tokenizer hashes, launch command, environment. A repair that changes any of these is a new experiment. E7 · B1
  • Checksummed evidence bundle. Raw request records, sensor logs, search history, failed attempts, native harness output, an independent recalculation script, SHA-256 index.
  • One source for every table. Web, Word, PDF, and UI charts regenerate from the same workbook. Automated checks reject unit mismatches, mismatched windows, reversed directions, min/max ordering, and stale labels. C6 · E1 · D2
  • Matching denominators. Wall energy integrated over the exact token window, GPU-only energy kept separate, tokens split into input, cached, reasoning, and output. D2 · E2 · E3
  • Every quantity labelled. Measured, calculated, or simulated, with the sensor, interval, and boundary. C7
  • The headline names its track. Controlled comparison, optimized deployment, or workload reproduction, with unavoidable differences listed beside the result.

Publication gate. A release contains a one-page answer to the sizing question, the exact workload and service contract, configuration and topology, all comparison curves, quality results, uncertainty, energy and cost accounting, limitations, and reproduction instructions. Until the planned tests run, the results section says "proposed experiment" and "expected diagnostic value". It is never filled with forecast improvements.

Proposed interactive gates for our own runs: p95 time to first token at most 2 s, p95 time per output token at most 50 ms, at least 99 percent successful requests. These are test contracts, not customer-approved requirements.
