Five AI performance papers, read the way a customer's engineer will read them

We reviewed five recent sponsored publications about AI performance on Dell platforms. Most of the measurements are useful. A few have problems that a skeptical customer will spot and that undercut the message. This brief says plainly which papers have a problem, why it matters, and how we would run the same tests so the result holds up.

We ran no new benchmarks for this review. Everything here comes from the papers' own published numbers, commands, and tables. Sponsorship is disclosed in each paper and is not itself a concern.

Dell PowerEdge R770 with RTX PRO 4500 · Qwen models

Good load curves, two errors in the write-up

The throughput curves are useful and the paper shares more setup detail than most. That detail is what exposes the two problems. Both are visible from the paper's own numbers, and both need fixing before the efficiency or configuration figures are quoted anywhere.

Our verdict

Correct before reusing

The throughput results may be fine. Two statements in the paper are not.

  • The tokens-per-watt figure is overstated four-fold.
  • The stated memory configuration cannot run the stated models.

Four GPUs' output, one GPU's power

Arithmetic error

What the paper saysIt divides the total token rate of 5,219 per second by the 151 watts of a single GPU and reports 34.5 tokens per watt. It then suggests the figure improves further with more GPUs.

It is like rating a car's fuel economy by counting the miles driven by the whole car but only the fuel burned by one of its four cylinders.

Why a customer will careTokens per watt is the number that goes into power budgets and total cost of ownership. A buyer who redoes this division, and someone will, gets a figure four times lower and stops trusting the rest of the paper.

What we would do instead
  • Measure electricity at the wall for the whole server, over exactly the same minutes in which the tokens were counted.
  • Report tokens per watt for the whole system, with GPU-only power shown separately.
  • Have one script recompute every table from the raw readings, so no figure can drift from its source.

A model that does not fit the card it is said to run on

Configuration error

What the paper saysThe methodology describes running 27B and 35B models at full 16-bit precision, one copy per 32 GB GPU.

The recipe calls for a 70-litre pot, and the kitchen only has 32-litre pots. Dinner was clearly served, so a different pot was used. The recipe just does not say which.

Why a customer will careA customer who orders this configuration and follows the write-up will not get the model to load. The results are probably real, but nobody can tell what setup produced them, so nobody can reproduce or size from them.

What we would do instead
  • Before every run, work out whether the model fits, then check that against what the GPU actually allocated.
  • Record the precision and settings the running software reports, not the model's name.
  • Treat any mismatch between the described setup and the running setup as a blocker for publication.
Prowess Consulting · Xeon 6 workload comparison

The numbers probably stand. The write-up does not.

Prowess published the actual commands it ran, which is more than most do. That is also why the problems are easy to find: one command does not do what the text claims, one headline figure differs between the PDF and the web page, and the biggest gain is mostly about having more cores, not faster ones.

Our verdict

Fix the write-up

Nothing suggests the measurements are false. A reader cannot repeat them from what was published, and one number is wrong in one of the two places it appears.

  • The CPU-pinning command is wrong as written.
  • 152% in the PDF became 52% on the web.
  • The 85% gain is more job slots, not a faster core.

"Reserve eight seats" was read as "reserve seat number eight"

Command error

What the paper saysThe molecular-dynamics test is described as running each job on a fixed set of cores, and the published launch command is meant to enforce that.

The command is the equivalent of telling a booking system "8" and expecting eight seats. The system reserves seat 8.

Why a customer will careAnyone who has used this tool will spot it immediately. If the published recipe does not match the experiment, the reader has to take the rest on faith, and the whole point of publishing commands was to avoid that.

What we would do instead
  • Write out the exact CPU list for every job and pin its memory to the matching part of the machine.
  • Record where every thread actually ran during the test, not just where it was told to run.
  • Publish the launcher that ran the jobs together, since that is the experiment that was measured.

More lanes on the highway, not faster cars

Needs clearer wording

What the paper saysThe newer system completes about 85 percent more work. The systems also have different core counts and memory. The paper does note that this is a whole-system result.

A thirty-lane highway moves more cars than a sixteen-lane one. That does not make any individual car faster.

Why a customer will careThe risk is downstream. When "85 percent more work" is shortened to "85 percent faster" in a slide, a customer expecting each job to finish faster will be disappointed. On this paper, the web page also shows 52 percent where the PDF shows 152 percent for one headline figure, which makes the same point: numbers that are retyped drift.

What we would do instead
  • Publish two numbers side by side: whole-system capacity, and a like-for-like comparison with the same number of cores and jobs.
  • Generate the web page, the PDF, and the slides from one data source so a figure cannot differ between them.
Principled Technologies · agentic AI on Dell hardware

Two numbers that do not agree with each other

PT discloses its workflows, software versions, and cost assumptions, and some of the workflows are genuinely agentic. But the headline cost chart says the opposite of its own appendix, and the five-year cloud bill implies far more work than the quoted speed would produce. Both are questions PT can answer. Until they do, the savings figure should not be quoted.

Our verdict

Reconcile before quoting savings

The measurements look real. The economics built on them cannot be checked from what is published.

  • The cost chart and the appendix table disagree.
  • The cost total and the speed figure describe very different amounts of work.

The chart says one thing, the appendix says the opposite

Labelling error

What the paper saysThe cost chart labels the cheaper per-token result as the less busy server. The appendix table assigns the labels the other way round.

A busier server spreads its purchase price over more work, so it should be cheaper per token. The appendix agrees. The chart, which is what gets copied into presentations, does not.

Why a customer will careThis is the economic headline of the paper. When a customer's finance team notices the chart contradicts the appendix, every other cost figure in the report gets questioned too.

What we would do instead
  • Generate every chart from the same table that appears in the appendix.
  • Run an automatic check before release that catches reversed labels, mismatched units, and stale captions.

A bill for 31 times more work than the speed figure would produce

Needs an explanation

What the paper saysIt quotes a five-year cloud bill of about $5.2 million at $0.90 per million tokens, and a system speed of 1,179 tokens per second.

It is a utility bill for thirty-one times more electricity than the meter says the house can draw. Perhaps the bill counts something the meter does not. The customer needs to be told what.

Why a customer will careThe savings versus cloud is the reason to read the paper. If the customer's own analyst cannot get from the speed to the bill, the comparison is unusable, however good the measurements are. A similar gap sits behind the paper's energy-per-token figure.

What we would do instead
  • Publish the token counts behind every cost figure, split into words read, words written, and cached input, per workflow.
  • State the load level and hours-per-day assumed in the bill.
  • Label a calculated cloud bill as a calculation, and only call it a comparison when the same work was actually run in both places.
Dell · Llama inference across GPU platforms

Sound measurements that prove less than they seem to

A useful comparison across request sizes and load levels, and unusually open about which runs were chosen as representative. No errors. The issue is what the results are taken to mean: several things changed between the platforms at the same time, and the load tests stopped while throughput was still climbing.

Our verdict

Sound data, narrower claim

Read it as "the best setup we found on each platform", not "this GPU is faster than that one". The paper is one sentence away from being right.

  • Precision, GPU, and parallel layout all changed together.
  • The top of the curve was never reached.

Three things changed, one got the credit

Claim broader than the test

What the paper saysIt compares Llama running in a compact 4-bit format on one GPU generation against an 8-bit format on another, with the way the model is split across GPUs left to vary.

Changing the engine, the fuel, and the gearbox at the same time, then crediting the engine for the faster lap.

Why a customer will careA customer wants to know what they get from the newer GPU. This test cannot tell them, and a competitor will say so. As a comparison of best available setups it is entirely fair; it just has to be described that way.

What we would do instead
  • Run two tracks and label each. One changes only the GPU and holds everything else fixed. The other gives each platform the same tuning effort and compares the best result.
  • List the unavoidable differences right beside the number, so the reader never has to hunt for them.

Testing stopped while the numbers were still climbing

Not a maximum

What the paper saysThe short-request throughput charts end at the highest load tested, with the line still rising.

Stopping the stopwatch while the runner is still accelerating, and calling that the top speed.

Why a customer will careSizing decisions come from the top of this curve. If the top was never found, the capacity figure is a floor, and it should be sold as one.

What we would do instead
  • Keep raising the load until the curve flattens, or say clearly that we stopped first and report the result as "at least this much".
  • Mark separately the highest load at which responses still arrive fast enough for a person waiting on them. That is the number a customer with real users needs.
Signal65 · long-context accuracy evaluation

A careful paper with one thing to tidy

This report says exactly what it tests and does not claim more. That restraint is its strength. We found no errors. One trend in it needs a small adjustment before it is quoted, and the study would be easier to repeat if its exact setup were published alongside it.

Our verdict

Sound, one request

Safe to cite for what it claims. Two things would make it stronger.

  • Recompute the trend across context lengths using only the models present at every length.
  • Publish the exact question set, settings, and per-question results.

The average at long lengths is over fewer models

Trend needs a small fix

What the paper saysIt shows how accuracy changes as documents get longer. Fewer models are able to take part at the longer lengths.

Like tracking a class's average grade across the school year while weaker students drop the course. The average can rise even if nobody learned anything.

Why a customer will careOnly if the trend is quoted as "accuracy holds up at long lengths". A customer whose model is one of the drop-outs will not see that result.

What we would do instead
  • Show the trend twice: once for all models that could take part, and once for the fixed group present at every length.
  • Publish the full per-question results and the exact setup, so anyone can rerun the study and get the same table.
How we work

Three habits that would have prevented every issue above

None of these problems came from bad hardware or dishonest people. They came from testing too little of the space, not knowing exactly what was running, or not leaving a trail. We build every study around the three habits that close those gaps.

Thorough

We test until the answer is bracketed, and we keep every result, including the ones that failed.

  • We push the load until the curve flattens. If we have to stop first, we say so and call the number a floor.
  • We test steady traffic and bursty traffic, because customers get both.
  • We pick a winner on a fresh set of runs, never on the best run we happened to see.
  • We run the finalist for hours, not minutes, and we count anything that times out or errors as work not done.

Expert

We know what is actually running, what limits it, and what a change will do, before we attribute a number to anything.

  • We check that the model fits and confirm what the software actually loaded, before the clock starts.
  • We map the machine: which cores, which memory, which GPUs talk to which, and where every thread ran.
  • We change one thing at a time, then combine the winners. Each platform gets the same tuning effort.
  • We separate "the most this box can do" from "the most it can do while people are waiting on it", and we label which one we are quoting.

Reproducible

Anyone can rerun our study, regenerate every chart, and get the same conclusions, including the unflattering ones.

  • Every run ships with its full recipe: hardware, firmware, software versions, model files, and the exact launch commands.
  • Every chart, table, and slide is generated from one data source, with automatic checks for reversed labels, mismatched units, and stale numbers.
  • Electricity is measured at the wall over the same minutes the work is counted, so efficiency figures always add up.
  • Raw readings, failed attempts, and a checksum index go in the evidence bundle. Nothing is quietly rerun until it looks good.

Our standard: a customer's own engineer can check every number and find nothing to correct.

That is the bar each paper above was held to. It is the bar we hold our own results to, including the throughput we have already measured on our eight-GPU host, which we describe as a measurement on one setup and not as a comparison with anyone until the matched test is run.

Proposed next step

Turn the strongest of these papers into one nobody can pick apart

We propose to rerun the Dell Llama workload on our own eight-GPU Dell host with capsim, our capacity-testing tool, and publish it with the full evidence chain. Same request sizes, same load levels, corrected accounting, and every setting disclosed.

1

Freeze and verify the setup

Record the machine, confirm the model fits, confirm what is actually running. Nothing is measured until the description and the reality match.

2

Reproduce the published workload

The same three request shapes at the same five load levels, run three times each, with the full grid published including any cell that failed.

3

Tune fairly and find the real top

Change one setting at a time under a fixed effort budget, then raise the load until the curve flattens. Report peak capacity and people-waiting capacity separately.

4

Publish the evidence, not just the chart

A one-page answer to the sizing question, wall-measured energy, an honest cost model, and a checksummed bundle anyone can audit.

What Dell gets. A performance publication whose headline says exactly what kind of comparison it is, whose every figure regenerates from the raw data, and whose limitations are stated before a competitor states them. The optional follow-ons are a quality check on long documents, a business-workflow test with real tasks scored right or wrong, and a like-for-like CPU study once matching systems are available.

Nothing on this page forecasts a performance gain. Where we found a plausible improvement, such as a better GPU layout or a tuning change, it is listed as an experiment to run, and the result will be reported with its uncertainty whether or not it favours the platform.
